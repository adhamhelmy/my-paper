\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This thesis presents a reinforcement learning fine-tuning framework for LLM-driven algorithmic
trading, training open-source large language models to generate executable Python trading
strategies via Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO).
We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, and Llama-3.1-8B and evaluate their strategy generation
performance and trading outcomes across 38 equities. 
Our results demonstrate that fine-tuning significantly enhances model performance, 
with an average increase of 82\% in strategy generation accuracy and a 250\% improvement in trading returns compared to base models. 
These findings underscore the potential of reinforcement learning fine-tuning in aligning LLM outputs with complex financial objectives, 
paving the way for more effective and adaptive algorithmic trading systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Financial Markets}
\label{sec:fin-markets}

  Financial markets are complex, nonlinear, and dynamic systems influenced by a wide range of factors.
The stochastic nature of price movements, combined with high volatility and non-stationarity, makes
modeling and decision-making in financial markets particularly challenging. As a result, statistical
and computational models have become essential tools for extracting meaningful information from market
data and supporting trading and investment decisions.

\section{Motivation for Intelligence}
\label{sec:motivation}

The complexity, uncertainty, and high dimensionality of financial markets motivate the use of
intelligent systems capable of learning, adaptation, and decision-making under uncertainty.
Traditional rule-based and static statistical models often struggle to cope with changing market
conditions. In contrast, intelligent methods---particularly those based on artificial
intelligence---can learn from data, capture nonlinear relationships, and adapt strategies in
response to evolving market dynamics.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

The goal of algorithmic trading is to exploit market volatility and price  fluctuations through timely buy and sell 
orders in order to grow investment capital. Approaches in this space broadly fall into two categories in the 
context of machine learning: \emph{prediction-based methods} and \emph{reinforcement learning-based methods}. 
This thesis focuses on the latter, framing the problem as follows.

Formally, given a financial instrument $s$ and a historical price series $\mathcal{D}_s = \{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_T\}$, 
the objective is to learn a policy $\pi_\theta$ that, conditioned on a prompt $p(s)$, generates an 
executable trading strategy $o \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid p(s))$ that maximises the expected annualised 
return when evaluated on an out-of-sample period:
\begin{equation}
    \max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{S}}\bigl[R(\pi_\theta, s)\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $R(\pi_\theta, s)$ denotes the annualised return of the generated strategy on symbol $s$, 
and $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of target financial instruments.

This thesis investigates the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does GRPO fine-tuning significantly improve a model's 
    ability to generate syntactically valid and executable trading strategies 
    compared to its base counterpart?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does fine-tuning improve the financial performance 
    of generated strategies in terms of annualised return and Sharpe ratio?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How does the effectiveness of GRPO fine-tuning 
    vary across model families and parameter scales?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Despite the rapid growth of research applying large language models (LLMs) to financial trading
and investment tasks, existing studies primarily rely on standard pretrained or fine-tuned LLMs
for prediction, sentiment analysis, or agent-based decision making. As far as our review
indicates, little to no work explicitly either incorporates LLMs to generate executable trading strategies or employs GRPO/RLVR to fine-tune them for this purpose.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The contributions of this thesis are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce a novel framework for trading strategy generation using large language models.
  \item We develop a backtesting environment to evaluate the trading strategies generated by LLMs on historical market data.
  \item We apply the Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO) algorithm to specialise a language
        model for trading strategy generation.
  \item We conduct empirical comparisons across multiple models and benchmarks, demonstrating the effectiveness of our
        approach in enhancing model performance on the strategy generation tasks.     
  \end{itemize}

\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

Algorithmic trading as a research area has gained considerable attention in recent years.
The agent's purpose is to exploit market fluctuations through timely buy and sell orders in
order to maximise capital returns. With the rise of machine learning, the use of intelligent,
adaptable systems in financial markets has become increasingly prevalent. In the context of
artificial intelligence, research on trading strategy design broadly follows two main
directions: \emph{prediction-based methods} and \emph{reinforcement learning-based methods}.

% ── 2.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Machine Learning and Prediction Models}
\label{sec:pred-models}


The goal of prediction models is to forecast the price of an asset
based on historical data, technical indicators, and sentiment analysis scores.
Using deep neural networks as universal function approximators, researchers have sought to
design architectures suited to financial time series. This has motivated the development of
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) \cite{rumelhart1986rnn} and their successor, Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) networks \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}, both of which are capable of capturing temporal
dependencies in sequential data.


The body of literature in this space is extensive \cite{huang2024novel}.
Studies \cite{zhang2021forecasting,qiao2022prediction,md2022novel}
used variations of LSTM and BiLSTM networks.
Hybrid models combining CNN, BiLSTM, and different attention mechanisms were employed in
\cite{lu2020cnn,huang2021financial,shi2023integrated}.
News headlines and sentiment analysis techniques were further integrated in
\cite{sridhar2021news}. These contributions represent only a fraction of the current research
efforts in this space.


Modelling financial markets is extremely difficult due to their high volatility, dynamic
nature, and inherent unpredictability. Moreover, predicting the market is only the first step
in this approach: practitioners must still construct trading strategies around these
predictions, adding further layers of complexity. Human intuition and theoretical assumptions
are typically required to bridge the gap between a price forecast and a concrete trading
decision. Critically, higher prediction accuracy does not guarantee higher returns
\cite{huang2024novel}. This motivates reinforcement learning, the second group of intelligent trading strategies.

% ── 2.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:rl}

Following AlphaGo's \cite{silver2016mastering} defeat of the top human player in the game
of Go, deep reinforcement learning gained significant attention from the research community.
In reinforcement learning \cite{sutton2018rl}, an agent sequentially interacts with its
environment with the goal of maximising cumulative reward. In the context of financial
trading, the environment is the stock market and the reward is some measure of investment
return.

At each time step the agent: (i) observes a \emph{state}, which may comprise historical
price data, technical indicators, or candlestick representations; (ii) takes an
\emph{action}, which may be a discrete buy/hold/sell signal, a continuous position size, or
a portfolio weight vector; and (iii) receives a \emph{reward}, which may be the raw return,
or a risk-adjusted metric such as the Sharpe ratio or the Sortino ratio. Traditional RL
algorithms are categorised into value-based, policy-based, and actor-critic methods.

\paragraph{Critic-Only (Value-Based)}
\label{sec:value-based}

In value-based methods, such as Temporal Difference learning \cite{sutton1988td} and
Q-Learning \cite{watkins1992qlearning}, the objective is to learn the action-value function
$Q(s, a)$---the expected cumulative return from taking action $a$ in state $s$ and thereafter
following the optimal policy. The optimal policy is derived indirectly by selecting the
action that maximises $Q(s, a)$ at each step. Implementations of value-based Deep
Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in single-asset trading include
\cite{carta2021,huang2023drl,taghian2022,theate2021,ye2023}.
Notably, value-based methods are the most widely adopted DRL approach for single-asset
trading strategies \cite{huang2024novel}.

\paragraph{Actor-Only (Policy-Based)}
\label{sec:policy-based}

Policy-based methods and policy gradient algorithms are formalised as
optimisation problems. Unlike value-based approaches, policy-based methods do not estimate a
value function; instead, they directly learn a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ that
maps states to a distribution over actions, optimising the policy parameters $\theta$ to
maximise the expected cumulative return \cite{sutton1999pg}. DRL trading strategies
implemented with policy-based methods include
\cite{lele2020,liu2021policy,wang2021policy}.

\paragraph{Actor-Critic}
\label{sec:actor-critic}

Actor-critic algorithms combine elements of both paradigms: an \emph{actor} maintains and
updates the policy, while a \emph{critic} estimates the value function. The actor selects
actions and the critic evaluates them, providing a lower-variance signal for policy updates
\cite{xiong2018}. This combination enhances both stability and learning efficiency
\cite{yang2020}. Actor-critic DRL has been applied to trading strategies in
\cite{ge2022,lima2021,liu2020actorcritic}.

\section{Large Language Models in Finance}
\label{sec:llms}

The advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has revolutionised natural language
processing and demonstrated remarkable potential across a wide range of domains, including
healthcare \cite{mehandru2024evaluating}, education \cite{zhang2024simulating}, and the financial
sector, which has attracted particularly significant research interest.

Since the public release of ChatGPT \cite{achiam2023gpt4}, research into LLM applications in stock and equity
markets has grown rapidly. A comprehensive survey \cite{jadhav2025llmequity} of 84
relevant papers categorises the work by end use into eight areas: stock price prediction,
sentiment analysis, automated trading, investment analysis, portfolio management, risk
management, financial data generation, and benchmarking.

\paragraph{Stock price forecasting.}
A significant body of work centres on predicting stock prices. Several studies
\cite{ni2024,ding2023,guo2024hauptmann,lopezlira2023}
integrate qualitative information---such as news and analyst reports---into their forecasting
pipelines, while \cite{valeyre2024,wang2024ts,wang2024b} focus on
time-series forecasting with LLMs.

\paragraph{Sentiment analysis.}
Research in this category focuses on extracting and interpreting sentiment from a range of
sources such as news, social media, and financial reports.
\cite{deng2022reddit,xing2024,kirtac2024}
extract sentiment insights from financial text, reflecting methodologies ranging from basic
lexical scoring to agent-based approaches.
Sentiment scoring is also featured in \cite{bond2023,lefort2024}, converting sentiment into
numerical signals that directly inform trading strategies.

\paragraph{Automated trading and decision systems.}
Many research efforts are dedicated to building autonomous trading systems
\cite{yu2024fincon,li2023tradinggpt,yu2023finmem,li2024cryptotrade}.
Some works additionally integrate sentiment analysis for trading and portfolio management
\cite{konstantinidis2024}.
\cite{saqur2024} incorporate reinforcement learning, while \cite{yang2024finrobot} provide
an agent platform that broadens access for researchers and practitioners.

\paragraph{Investment and equity research.}
\cite{papasotiriou2024,li2023multimodal,zhou2024finrobot} use LLMs to
automate investment and equity research and analysis.
\cite{fatouros2024} propose a framework for stock selection, which \cite{fatouros2025}
extends and optimises for portfolio management.
\cite{wang2023alphagpt} models researchers trading strategy proposals, while \cite{wang2024b} models investor
behaviour.
A further strand focuses on explainability, developing frameworks to enhance model
interpretability \cite{koa2024explainable,lopezlira2023,tong2024,zhao2024portfolio} and
comparing interpretability across general-purpose LLMs \cite{abdelsamie2024}.

\paragraph{Portfolio management.}
\cite{ko2024,huang2024portfolio,zhao2024portfolio} focus on
portfolio management using approaches ranging from traditional optimisation methods to
reinforcement learning.
Portfolio management advice is explored by \cite{fieberg2024,lu2025}.

\paragraph{Risk management.}
\cite{park2024,koa2024temporal} addressed the reliability of LLMs in equity markets and the identification of
unusual market conditions or portfolio crashes.

\paragraph{Financial content generation and data integration.}
\cite{nishida2025,pop2024} automate the production of financial writing such as news articles
and equity research reports.
\cite{liu2023fingpt} introduce FinGPT, a framework for democratising access to financial LLMs.

\paragraph{Benchmarking and surveys.}
\cite{zhao2024llmsurvey,nie2024survey,ding2024survey}
offer broad overviews of LLM applications in finance.
\cite{li2024investorbench,mateega2025} focus on benchmark development, enabling
more standardised comparisons and evaluations of LLM performance in financial tasks.
\cite{abdelsamie2024} benchmark specialised financial LLMs against human analysts and
general-purpose models.
\cite{bi2024,chiang2025} examine the practical potential and limitations of LLMs,
underscoring the need for ongoing evaluation in this rapidly evolving field.

Within this categorization, our approach falls broadly under Investment and equity research, since we prompt our model to generate trading strategies instead of making explicit trading decisions.

\section{LLMs in Financial Trading}
\label{sec:llm-trading}

As previously outlined, LLMs offer substantial potential across financial market applications. With
a focus on trading specifically, implementations are broadly categorised \cite{ding2024survey}
into two paradigms: \emph{LLMs as traders}, which directly generate buy, hold, or sell
decisions from inputs such as price data, news, and financial reports and \emph{LLMs as alpha miners}, which generate alpha
factors---quantitative signals encoding predictive information---rather than explicit trading decisions.

\subsection{LLMs as Traders}
\label{sec:llm-traders}
Many studies incorporating LLMs as traders were mentioned in \cref{sec:llms}. Additionally, \cite{ding2024survey} further highlights 
subcategories of LLM-based trading systems.

\paragraph{News-Driven.}

Financial sentiment analysis of stock news, financial updates and sentiment scores are incorporated in the trading strategy \cite{lopezlira2023, wu2024llmnews}.
Further research, demonstrated performance enhancement with LLMs fine-tuned with financial related dataset aligning LLMs with domain-specific knowledge \cite{kirtac2024, zhang2024chinese}.
More advanced architectures, involved summarisation and reasoning to identify and analyse relationship between historical news and corresponding price movement \cite{fatouros2024, wang2024llmfactor}.

\paragraph{Reflection-Driven.}

Reflection is built on extracted memory using summarization of high-level knowledge and insights, are progressively aggregated from raw memories and observations \cite{park2023generative}.
The raw inputs are summarized into memories, relevant memories are retrieved and integrated with new observations to produce reflections, which are then utilized to generate trading decisions \cite{yu2023finmem}.
\cite{zhang2024finagent} further proposes a multimodal agent that takes in numeric, text and image data and incorporates technical indicators and a human analyst to more effectively capture market dynamics.

\paragraph{Debate-Driven.}

Debating among LLMs which has proven to be an effective method to enhance the reasoning and factual validity, is also widely adopted in LLM financial agents.
\cite{xing2024} proposed a debating framework where LLM agents with different roles debate each other, improving news sentiment classification performance.
\cite{li2023tradinggpt} proposed a similar architecture to \cite{yu2023finmem} with an additional step that agents debate each other’s actions and reflections, improving robustness of reflections.

\paragraph{Reinforcement Learning Driven.}

Reinforcement learning methods, have proven effective in aligning LLM outputs with expected behaviors \cite{ouyang2022instructgpt, lee2023rlaif}.
Backtesting provides a cost-effective method for generating high-quality feedback on trading decisions that can serve as rewards.
\cite{koa2024explainable} proposed leveraging reinforcement learning with memorization and reflection modules, utilizing a series of predictions derived from financial market history to refine predictions in real-world markets.
\cite{ding2023} developed a RL-based framework, where an LLM is used to generate embeddings from news headlines, which are then projected into the stock feature space.

\subsection{LLM as an Alpha Miner}
\label{sec:llm-alphaminer}

Another effective approach is using LLMs as Alpha Miners, where the LLM generates alpha factors instead of directly making trading decisions. 
\cite{wang2024quantagent} demonstrated this by introducing a principled framework, comprising of a two-layer loop. In the inner loop,
an agent takes in a general idea from a human trader and generates a script as its implementation.
Another agent provides feedback to refine the script, the agent refines its responses by drawing from its knowledge base.
While in the outer loop, committed code is backtested in real-world scenarios and the results are used to automatically enhance the knowledge base with new insights,
enabling the agent to progressively approximate optimal behaviour with provable efficiency. In a subsequent research,
\cite{wang2023alphagpt} propose a human in the loop on a similar architecture and experimental environment, 
to provide a heuristic way to ``understand'' the ideas of quant researchers and outputs creative, insightful, and effective alphas. 
Both studies demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of the LLM powered alpha mining, a conventionally resource intensive job, 
via a number of alpha mining experiments.



\section{Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT)}
\label{sec:rft}

Fine-tuning is the adaptation of a pretrained model to a specific task or domain by further training it on a smaller, task-specific dataset.
\cite{liu2023fingpt} introduces FinGPT, an open-sourced framework that adapts general-purpose LLMs for applications,
including robo-advisory and sentiment analysis for algorithmic trading using LoRA \cite{hu2021lora}/QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora} fine-tuning coupled
with inherent feedback from the market, dubbed Reinforcement Learning with Stock Prices (RLSP). 
Parameter efficient finetuning (PEFT) aims to reduce the computational and memory costs
associated with finetuning large models.
LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} and QLoRA \cite{dettmers2023qlora}  are parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods that enable the adaptation of large models with a significantly reduced number of trainable parameters, 
making it feasible to fine-tune large LLMs on limited computational resources.

Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) adapts a pretrained language model using a scalar feedback
signal. Unlike supervised fine-tuning, the model is not trained on labelled correct answers;
instead, an algorithmic grader scores the model's outputs, and the model's weights are updated
to increase the likelihood of high-scoring responses and suppress low-scoring ones. By
generating responses to prompts and grading each result, the model's chain-of-thought (CoT)
reasoning is reinforced for outputs the grader deems correct or desirable. This approach is
particularly effective for domain-specific tasks \cite{openai2024rft} and enables
alignment of model behaviour to objectives such as stylistic consistency, safety, or---most
relevantly here---domain accuracy in financial decision-making.

While some studies use reinforcement learning as a module or part of the framework,
such as \cite{yu2023finmem, yu2024fincon, gu2024margin}; others incorporate reinforcement fine-tuning,
directly updating model weights via RL gradients to adapt and align the model to financial
trading tasks \cite{saqur2024, ding2023, koa2024explainable}.

\cite{saqur2024} introduces Reinforcement Learning from Market Feedback (RLMF),
a regime-adaptive market execution method that uses natural market price signals as rewards
to update the weights of a Llama-2 7B model, enabling continuous adaptation to evolving
market dynamics.
\cite{ding2023} proposes a Self-Correlated Reinforcement Learning (SCRL) framework in which
a Llama 7B is fine-tuned via PPO using an Actor-Critic setup, aligning LLM-generated
news embeddings with stock feature representations through iterative RL-driven weight updates.
\cite{koa2024explainable} introduces the Summarize-Explain-Predict (SEP) framework, which
applies PPO to fine-tune an LLM to generate self-reflective explanations for past stock
movements, improving both prediction accuracy and portfolio construction performance.

\section{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

The overarching objective of reinforcement learning is to increase the probability of
observing \emph{good} outcomes and decrease the probability of observing \emph{bad} ones.
In our setting this is operationalised as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \emph{environment} is the financial market.
  \item The \emph{action} is the trading strategy generated by the model in response to a prompt.
  \item A \emph{reward} is the return generated by the strategy when back-tested on historical data.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike supervised learning, the optimal action cannot be known in advance; the agent
must infer it from the observed consequences of its decisions.


\subsection{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)}
\label{sec:rlhf}

OpenAI popularised Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
\cite{christiano2017rlhf}, in which an agent is trained to produce outputs that are
rated as more useful by human evaluators. The state is a prompt, the action is the
model's generated response, and the reward is a human preference score. A \emph{reward
model} is trained to approximate these human judgements, and a policy is then optimised
against it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/RLHF.png}
  \caption{RLHF training loop. Source: \cite{unsloth2024}.}
  \label{fig:rlhf}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)}
\label{sec:ppo}

To implement RLHF in practice, Schulman et al.~\cite{schulman2017ppo} introduced Proximal Policy
Optimisation (PPO). In this framework the system consists of three components: a
\emph{generating policy} $\pi_\theta$ (the model being trained), a \emph{reference
policy} $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (a frozen copy of the original model), and a
\emph{value model} that estimates the expected reward from a given state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/PPO.png}
  \caption{PPO training loop. Source: \cite{unsloth2024}.}
  \label{fig:ppo}
\end{figure}

The PPO objective is

\begin{equation}
  J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min\!\left(
    \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a \mid s)}\,A,\;
    \operatorname{clip}\!\left(
      \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a \mid s)},\,
      1 - \varepsilon,\, 1 + \varepsilon
    \right) A
  \right)\right],
  \label{eq:ppo}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A$ is the advantage estimate and $\varepsilon$ is a small clipping
hyperparameter. The clipping term prevents excessively large policy updates, promoting
training stability. An additional KL-divergence penalty with coefficient $\beta > 0$
further constrains the policy from deviating too far from the reference.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO)}
\label{sec:grpo}

DeepSeek introduced Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO) \cite{shao2024deepseekmath}
and applied it to train their R1 reasoning models \cite{deepseekr1}. GRPO makes two key simplifications over PPO:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The \emph{value model} is removed entirely.
  \item The \emph{learned reward model} is replaced by a custom, algorithmically
        verifiable reward function---a paradigm known as Reinforcement Learning with
        Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
\end{itemize}

These changes substantially reduce memory usage and computational cost, since PPO
requires training and maintaining both a reward model and a value model in addition to
the policy.

In place of the value model, GRPO estimates the advantage by sampling the policy $G$
times for the same prompt and computing the Z-score of the resulting rewards:

\begin{equation}
  A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_G)}
             {\operatorname{std}(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_G)},
  \label{eq:grpo-advantage}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $r_i$ is the reward assigned to the $i$-th sample. This group-relative
normalisation replaces the critic network with a statistical estimate derived directly
from the reward function, requiring no additional learned components.

RLVR is particularly well-suited to tasks with easily verifiable solutions, such as
mathematics and code generation. Although financial strategy evaluation is more nuanced,
it is nonetheless \emph{verifiable}: a generated strategy either produces positive
returns when back-tested or it does not. This makes GRPO an appropriate training
algorithm for our setting.

  \caption{GRPO training loop. Source: \cite{unsloth2024}.}
  \label{fig:grpo}
\end{figure}

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system}

fine-tune the language model. Unsloth supports several reinforcement fine-tuning
algorithms, including GRPO, GSPO, and DPO. The pipeline operates as follows; a prompt is fed to the LLM, 
which generates a trading strategy; the backtesting module evaluates the strategy and returns a reward signal to update the model via GRPO.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{GRPO Fine-Tuning for Trading Strategy Generation}
\label{alg:grpo-training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Pre-trained LLM $\pi_{\theta}$, symbol set $\mathcal{S}$, training steps $T$, group size $G$, clip ratio $\varepsilon$, numerical-stability constant $\delta$
\Ensure Fine-tuned LLM $\pi_{\theta^{*}}$

\For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
  \State Sample symbol $s \sim \mathcal{S}$ and construct prompt $p(s)$
  \State Generate $G$ strategy completions $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\} \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid p(s))$

  \For{each completion $o_i$}
    \State $r_i \gets \textsc{Backtest}(o_i, s)$ \Comment{returns reward per \cref{tab:reward}}
  \EndFor

  \State Compute group baseline: $\bar{r} \gets \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} r_i$
  \State Normalise advantages: $\hat{A}_i \gets \dfrac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma(r) + \delta}$

  \State Compute GRPO objective:
  \[
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      \min\!\left(\rho_i\,\hat{A}_i,\;
                  \mathrm{clip}(\rho_i, 1{-}\varepsilon, 1{+}\varepsilon)\,\hat{A}_i\right)
      - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta} \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)
  \]
  where $\rho_i = \dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(o_i \mid p)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_i \mid p)}$

  \State Update $\theta \gets \theta + \alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\pi_{\theta^{*}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Back-testing.}
Once the model produces an output, the strategy class is extracted via regex and passed
to our back-testing setup. \texttt{Backtrader} \cite{backtrader2024} is a
Python-based strategy back-testing framework. Look-ahead bias---in which a strategy
inadvertently accesses future price data during simulation---is explicitly prohibited by
the prompt constraints and enforced by the Backtrader execution environment.

\paragraph{Reward function.}
The back-test result is mapped to a scalar reward according to the following hierarchy,
ordered from worst (most penalised) to best:

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Reward function: outcome categories ranked from worst to best.}
  \label{tab:reward}
  \begin{tabular}{clc}
    \toprule
    Rank & Outcome & Reward \\
    \midrule
    1 & No strategy generated, or class has empty methods & $-10$ (heavy penalty) \\
    2 & Syntactically invalid code                        & $-3$ \\
    3 & Strategy throws a runtime error during back-test  & $-2$ \\
    4 & Strategy generates no trading signals             & $-1$ \\
    5 & Strategy results in negative returns              & $0$  \\
    6 & Strategy results in positive returns              & $R$ (annual return) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Outcomes 1--3 are penalised to discourage invalid outputs; 
the model must learn to produce syntactically and semantically correct Backtrader strategies before any financial objective can be pursued, as the model needs to learn to generate valid strategies. 
Therefore, outcome 1 is heavily penalised as not generating a strategy is the worst-case scenario, followed by outcomes 2 and 3. 
Negative returns are assigned a reward of zero rather than a negative value: while a strategy with negative returns is undesirable, it
is strictly better than one that is syntactically invalid or generates no signals at
all. For positive returns, the reward is set equal to the annualised return $R$, so that
higher returns receive proportionally higher rewards, incentivising the model to
discover increasingly profitable strategies.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The model was trained on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80GB of VRAM. We used the \texttt{Unsloth} library \cite{unsloth2024} for inference and
fine-tuning the language model. The model was trained for 500 steps, with a learning rate of 5e-6, weight decay of 0.01, 
adam optimizer and gradient accumulation steps set to 8. Model was tested before and after training to evaluate the improvement in strategy generation performance.
The training process was monitored using Weights \& Biases \cite{wandb2024}, which provided real-time tracking of training metrics, rewards, and model outputs. 

\section{Dataset}
The proposed method was trained on historical stock prices of 38 US stocks, 
the 30 constituents of the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) index, 
in addition to 8 additional stocks AMZN, COIN, GE, GOOGL, NFLX, NIO, TSLA, UVV, for the ten years from 2012-01-01 to 2021-12-31.
The data was sourced from Alpha Vantage \cite{alphavantage2024}, a provider of free stock APIs.
The data was preprocessed to ensure compatibility with the Backtrader framework. 
The testing dataset spans from 2022-01-01 to 2025-12-31, to evaluate model performance and from 2022-06-01 to 2024-01-01 to compare against several baselines and benchmarks from the literature.
\section{Evaluation Metrics}
We employ a widely used set of financial performance metrics to evaluate the generated trading
strategies and compare against several baselines from the literature.
These metrics include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cumulative Return (CR):} CR measures the total return on an investment over a
  specified period, representing the cumulative effect of all returns since the start of the
  investment horizon. Let $W_T$ denote the final portfolio value and $W_0$ the initial capital:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{CR} = \frac{W_T - W_0}{W_0} \times 100
  \end{equation}

  \item \textbf{Annualised Return (AR):} AR is the annualised average profit-and-loss (\%)
  provided by an investment during trading.  
  \begin{equation}
    \text{AR} = \left[\left(1 + \text{CuR}\right)^{\frac{252}{\text{Days Held}}} - 1\right] \times 100
  \end{equation} where $\text{CuR} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}
  \frac{C_t - C_{t-1}}{C_{t-1}}$

  \item \textbf{Sharpe Ratio (ShR):} ShR measures the average excess return per unit of total
  risk. Let $\mathbb{E}\{R_p\}$ be the expected portfolio return, $R_f$ the risk-free rate, and
  $\sigma_p$ the portfolio standard deviation. We assume $R_f = 0$:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{ShR} = \frac{\mathbb{E}\{R_p\} - R_f}{\sigma_p}
  \end{equation}

  \item \textbf{Maximum Drawdown (MDD):} MDD captures the largest peak-to-trough decline in
  portfolio value before a new peak is reached, measuring worst-case loss experienced over the
  evaluation period:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{MDD} = \max_{t \in [0,T]} \frac{\displaystyle\max_{\tau \in [0,t]} W_\tau - W_t}{\displaystyle\max_{\tau \in [0,t]} W_\tau}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\section{Baseline Methods}
\label{sec:baselines}
To contextualise the performance of our GRPO-trained model, we compare our model against baseline, reinforcement learning-based, and LLM-based methods from the literature. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Traditional rule-based strategies}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{Buy \& Hold (B\&H):} a passive benchmark that holds the asset for the entire
            evaluation period.
    \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Machine-learning-based methods}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{LSTM:} a recurrent network trained to predict next-step returns, with positions
            determined by the sign of the prediction.
    \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Reinforcement-learning-based methods}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo}:} Proximal Policy Optimisation trained directly
            on the price series via the FinRL framework \cite{liu2021finrl}.
      \item \emph{DQN \cite{mnih2015human}:} Deep Q-Network adapted for discrete trading actions.
    \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{LLM-based methods}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{FinGPT \cite{yang2023fingpt}:} an open-source LLM framework for converting
            financial news and prices into financial decisions.
      \item \emph{FinMem \cite{yu2023finmem}:} an advanced LLM agent framework for automated
            trading, fine-tuned to boost investment returns.
      \item \emph{FinAgent \cite{zhang2024finagent}:} a general-purpose LLM agent platform for
            financial applications, including trading strategy generation and execution.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:hyperparameters}
The GRPO training was conducted with the following hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Max Sequence Length:} $2048$
  \item \textbf{Quantisation:} $4$-bit
  \item \textbf{LoRA Rank:} $32$
  \item \textbf{LoRA Alpha:} $64$
  \item \textbf{Temperature:} $1.0$ 
  \item \textbf{Learning Rate:} $5 \times 10^{-6}$
  \item \textbf{Weight Decay:} $0.01$
  \item \textbf{Optimizer:} AdamW (8-bit)
  \item \textbf{Gradient Accumulation Steps:} $8$
  \item \textbf{Number of Generations:} $2$
  \item \textbf{Group Size (G):}  $16$
\end{itemize}

These hyperparameters follow established practice for reinforcement fine-tuning 
of language models \cite{unsloth2024, openai2024rft}. The LoRA rank of $32$ was 
chosen to provide sufficient capacity for domain-specific code generation, with 
alpha set to twice the rank following the standard doubling convention \cite{hu2021lora}. 
A temperature of $1.0$ was used to encourage strategy diversity during training. 
The effective group size $G = 16$ is the product of gradient accumulation steps 
($8$) and number of generations per step ($2$).

\chapter{Conclusion}

\section{Summary}

In this thesis, we explored the application of Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRPO) to train large language models for financial trading strategy generation. 
We implemented a training pipeline that prompts the model to generate Backtrader-compatible trading strategies, evaluates them via back-testing, and assigns rewards based on their performance. 
Our experiments demonstrated that GRPO fine-tuning significantly improves the model's ability to generate valid and profitable trading strategies compared to its base counterparts. 
The fine-tuned models achieved the highest profitable rate and mean reward among all models tested. 
Our results suggest that reinforcement fine-tuning with GRPO is an effective method for aligning large language models with complex, domain-specific objectives such as financial trading strategy generation.

\section{Limitations}

While our approach shows promise, there are several limitations to consider. 
First, the reward function is relatively simple and may not capture all relevant aspects of strategy performance, such as risk-adjusted returns or drawdown characteristics. 
Second, the back-testing environment is based on historical data, which may not fully reflect real-world market conditions or account for factors like slippage and transaction costs. 
Third, our training process is computationally intensive, which limited our capacity to experiment with larger models or longer training durations. 
Finally, while GRPO simplifies the training pipeline by removing the need for a value model and learned reward model, it may still require careful tuning of hyperparameters and sufficient sampling to ensure stable training.

\section{Future Work}

Future research could explore more sophisticated reward functions that incorporate additional performance metrics or risk measures.
Enhancing the back-testing environment to include more realistic market conditions, such as incorporating transaction costs, could provide a more accurate assessment of strategy performance. 
Experimenting with larger models or longer training durations could further improve the model's ability to generate profitable strategies.
Additionally, investigating alternative RLHF algorithms, such as DPO or GSPO, could provide insights into the most effective training methods for this task. 
Finally, applying this framework to multi-asset strategies or incorporating additional data sources, such as news sentiment or macroeconomic indicators, could further enhance the model's trading capabilities.
